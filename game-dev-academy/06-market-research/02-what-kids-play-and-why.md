# What Kids Play, and Why

> **What you'll learn**
> - What kids aged 6–17 actually play, on what devices, and for how long (2024–2026 data).
> - What research says makes a game fun for them (friends first) and what makes them quit.
> - An age-band table (6–8, 9–12, 13–17) you can design against.

**Tags:** **[P]** the company or body said it · **[S]** analyst, survey firm or reputable press · **[U]** unverified. As of Oct 2026.

> **Honest warning:** good, recent, *global* data on kids' gaming is thin. Most surveys are US, UK or Australia, many are parent-reported, and several are commissioned by companies. Each number below says where it's from. Don't stretch a UK number into a global fact.

---

## 1. The big picture in six facts

1. **Almost all kids game.** 88% of UK 3–17s play games on some device; 72% of 8–17s game online (Ofcom, 2026) [P] ([Ofcom Growing Up Gaming, Oct 13 2026](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/protecting-children/growing-up-gaming-research-insights); [Ofcom Children & Parents report, May 21 2026](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/media-habits-children/childrens)).
2. **Gaming is the #1 free-time activity** UK 8–17s pick (43%), ahead of TV/films (36%) [P] (Ofcom, May 2026, same).
3. **Roblox is the default for under-13s.** In Australia, Roblox play rises from 41% of 6–7s to 70% of 12–13s (Roy Morgan, Apr–Dec 2025) [S] ([Roy Morgan](https://www.roymorgan.com/findings/10136-yas-roblox-for-young-australians-survey-december-2025)). Childwise (UK) found Roblox the top mobile-gaming choice for 7–12s [S] ([Kidscreen, Oct 2025](https://kidscreen.com/2025/10/17/which-roblox-games-are-kids-favorites/)).
4. **Roblox's own users are mostly under 18.** Of age-checked Roblox users (end of Q2 2026): **35% under 13, 38% aged 13–17, 27% 18+** [P] ([Roblox Q2 2026 letter](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001315098/000162828026051059/ex991-robloxq22026earnin.htm)).
5. **YouTube is how they find games.** 85% of US 6–15s discover new games through YouTube videos (parent-reported, WildBrain/Savanta, n=2,091 parents) [S] ([Kidscreen, Mar 2025](https://kidscreen.com/2025/03/05/report-families-are-bonding-over-video-games/)).
6. **Friends are the point.** 89% of US teen gamers play with other people; 47% have made a friend online through a game (Pew, fieldwork fall 2023) [S] ([Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/2024/05/09/teens-and-video-games-today/)).

### Devices

- **US teens** play on: console 73%, smartphone 70%, computer 49%, tablet 33%, VR 24% [S] (Pew, fall 2023).
- **Roblox overall** is majority mobile. In June 2026, Roblox was the **most downloaded mobile game worldwide**, ahead of Free Fire (Sensor Tower) [S] ([Sensor Tower June 2026](https://sensortower.com/blog/top-10-worldwide-mobile-games-by-revenue-and-downloads-in-june-2026)).
- Younger kids lean on tablets; teens on phones and consoles. We found no recent global device split for 6–12s (gap; see §9).

## 4. Spending and parental controls

- **Most kids don't spend.** In five big European markets, 76% of parents of child gamers say their child makes no in-game purchases; among those who do, 95% of parents monitor spending; average spend fell from €39 to **€31/month** (Ipsos for Video Games Europe, 2024 data) [S] ([Video Games Europe](https://www.videogameseurope.eu/publication/in-game-purchases-in-european-markets-2024/)). A 2025 wave reportedly shows 72% non-spenders and spending down another 13% [U] (secondary write-ups only).
- **Roblox:** 27M monthly unique payers vs 123M daily users in Q2 2026 [P] (Q2 letter). Roblox said per-hour spending fell **most among younger US/Canada players** [P].
- **Roblox gift cards** were the top US retail game product by dollars in 2025 (Circana) [S] ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/circana-the-us-gaming-market-in-december25)). Lots of kid spending is **a parent buying a card**, so the purchase decision is shared.
- **Parental rules (UK, Ofcom Oct 2026)** [P]: 56% of parents set time limits, 51% set when children can play, only **43% set rules about who they play with**, 9% set no rules.
- **Roblox controls (2026)** [P]: Roblox Kids (5–8): Minimal/Mild games that passed a selection process, **all chat off by default**. Roblox Select (9–15): chat only with similar ages. About 30,000 games were available to these accounts by Q2 2026. Parents can set spend limits.

**Design implications:**
- Your game must be fun for the **~3 in 4 kids who never pay**.
- Rate your game honestly (Minimal/Mild) and keep it clean so it qualifies for **Roblox Kids/Select catalogs**, or you lose 5–15-year-olds.
- Never design around a child tapping "buy" without a parent. Clear prices in Robux, no fake timers. See `07-engagement-and-retention/03-ethics-and-law-for-kids-games.md`.

## 5. Social play: friends are the product

- 89% of teen gamers play with others; 47% made a friend through a game; "spending time with others" is a reason to play for ~3 in 4 teens (Pew) [S].
- Young Fortnite players prefer multiplayer to solo, and friends to strangers (Carter et al. 2020, summarized by Digital Wellness Lab, Sep 2023) [S] ([Digital Wellness Lab](https://digitalwellnesslab.org/research-briefs/childrens-views-on-gaming/)).
- 55% of UK kids who game online play with people they've never met at least sometimes; parents' top worry is strangers (64%) [P] (Ofcom, Oct 2026).

**Design implications:** make it easy to **play with a friend you already know** (invite links, party join, co-op waves) and hard to be contacted by strangers. That's what kids and parents both want.

## 6. YouTube, TikTok and memes

- 85% of US 6–15s find games through YouTube (parent-reported) [S] (WildBrain/Savanta 2025).
- Gaming is the top YouTube category for Australian 6–13 viewers (53%) [S] ([Roy Morgan](https://www.roymorgan.com/findings/10131-yas-youtube-for-young-australians-survey-december-2025)).
- 80% of kids say they've asked for something after an influencer talked about it (Precise TV, 2025) [S] ([Net Influencer](https://www.netinfluencer.com/youtube-captures-78-percent-of-gen-alpha-viewers-research-shows/)).
- **The 2025 "Italian brainrot" wave** started as AI-made TikTok memes and became **Steal a Brainrot**, which set a Roblox record of **25,868,678 concurrent players on Oct 11, 2025** (Guinness) [S] ([Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/503322-most-concurrent-players-for-a-videogame-made-in-roblox)). The humour works *because* adults don't get it [S] ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/09/07/what-is-italian-brain-rot-gen-z-social-media-tiktok)).

**Design implications:**
- Build moments that are **fun to watch**: big reveals, funny fails, rare drops, a "boss wave" kids can clip.
- Memes can lift a game, but they fade, and Roblox's 2026 algorithm now rewards **28-day retention** [P]. A meme should be the *hook*, not the whole game.

## 7. What kids say makes a game fun (research-backed)

| Driver | Evidence | What it looks like in a TD game |
|---|---|---|
| **Friends / social** | Pew: social play near-universal; Digital Wellness Lab review [S] | 2–4 player co-op, shared win, trade/show off towers |
| **Plain fun & humour** | 87% of teens say fun is a major reason to play (Pew) [S]; brainrot memes [S] | Silly enemies, funny sounds, jokes on loss screens |
| **Creativity** | Kids value Minecraft's freedom and imagination (Livingstone & Pothong 2021, via DWL) [S] | Custom tower skins, base decoration, map editor |
| **Collecting** | Top Roblox favourites (Adopt Me!, Grow a Garden, Steal a Brainrot) are built around collecting [S] (Roy Morgan, Childwise) | Unit index, rarities with **shown** odds, evolutions |
| **Competition & mastery** | ~2 in 3 teens say competing is a reason to play (Pew) [S] | Leaderboards, timed challenges, harder difficulty tiers |
| **Identity / status** | Game knowledge affects boys' standing with friends (Scholes et al. 2022, via DWL) [S] | Titles, badges, rare cosmetics earned by skill |
| **Short sessions** | Phones + school days = short gaps. Measured session length: unknown for your game; check Roblox analytics | 3–8 minute rounds, progress saved every wave |

## 8. What makes kids quit

Direct research on kids' own reasons for quitting is **weak**. Most studies ask parents. What we can say:

- **Friends leave → they leave.** Social play is the main reason to stay (Pew), so the reverse holds: no friends online, no reason to log in. [S, inferred]
- **Bad first minute.** Roblox now measures **first-play bounce rate** and **play-through rate** in recommendations [P] (Q2 2026 letter). Roblox thinks the first session matters enough to rank games on it.
- **Harassment.** 43% of teen gamers have been called names, threatened or sent explicit content (Pew) [S].
- **Sleep and time conflicts.** 41% of teens say gaming hurts their sleep; 38% have chosen to cut back (Pew) [S]. Parents limit time (56%, Ofcom) [P].
- **Unfairness/greed.** Kids and parents call out pay-to-win and gambling-like mechanics loudly. In Jan 2026 Fortnite's *Steal the Brainrot* island drew a backlash over paid spin wheels aimed at kids [S] ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Fortnite-players-slam-Steal-the-Brainrot-microtransactions-as-gambling.1205005.0.html)).
- **Reading walls and confusing UI.** Our working rule from playtests, not a measured stat: if a 7-year-old has to read a paragraph, they leave. Test it (see `04-how-to-research-a-game-idea.md`).

## 9. Age-band design table

Preferences are from the data above. **Reading level and attention notes are design guidance, not measured numbers.** Test with real kids before trusting them.

| | **6–8** | **9–12** | **13–17** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Roblox account type** [P] | Roblox Kids (5–8): Minimal/Mild games only, no chat | Roblox Select (9–15): chat with similar ages | Select until 15; 16+ standard account |
| **What they like** | Cute characters, pets, colours, collecting, simple goals, silly humour | Collecting + trading, friends, competition, memes, YouTube-famous games | Competition, skill, status, fashion/identity, social hangouts, humour |
| **Reading** | Early readers. Icons, pictures, voice/sound cues; 1–3 word labels | Can read short sentences; still skip text. One-line tips | Read fine but won't read much. Keep text short anyway |
| **Attention** | Short rounds; instant feedback every few seconds; avoid long waits | Will play longer if friends are there and goals are clear | Long sessions for mastery; bored fast by grind with no skill |
| **Social** | Play next to siblings/friends; chat off by default, so use emotes and pings | Friends are the main reason to log in; invites and co-op matter | Squads, rivals, leaderboards, showing off |
| **Spending** | Parent decides; mostly doesn't pay | Asks parents; gift cards; wants what YouTubers show | Some own money; judges value hard; hates pay-to-win |
| **Design implications** | Tap-only controls, big buttons, no failure spirals, no stranger contact, Minimal content label | Clear collection goals, fair shown odds, co-op waves, clip-worthy moments, weekly updates | Hard modes, ranked play, cosmetics that show skill, creator/YouTube hooks |
| **Danger zones** | Reading walls, scary content, any pressure to buy | FOMO pressure, trading scams, stranger contact | Toxic chat, pay-to-win, feeling "talked down to" |

## ✏️ Exercise (one sitting)

1. Pick your current game. Write which **one** age band it's mainly for.
2. Using the table, list **3 things in your game that break** that band's rules (e.g. a 40-word tutorial popup for 6–8s).
3. Watch 2 YouTube videos of a top game in your genre made *for* that age. Note the moments the YouTuber reacts loudest.
4. Fix one broken thing and add one "clip-worthy" moment this week.

## 🤖 Train the Model

**What the model should learn**
- Recommend design choices **by age band**, grounded in data (friends, collecting, humour, short sessions), with sources and tags.
- Treat kid spending and social features **ethically**: free kids must have fun; no stranger-contact designs; no pressure to buy.
- Be honest that kids' data is thin, parent-reported and regional, and suggest how to measure (analytics, playtests).

**Dataset:** `06-market-research/train/kids.jsonl`

**How to check it:** `06-market-research/train/kids.eval.jsonl`. A good answer:
- names the age band and changes advice to fit it,
- puts friends/social play near the top,
- cites at least one tagged source for any number,
- never recommends manipulative monetization to children,
- says when the data is missing and how to test instead.
